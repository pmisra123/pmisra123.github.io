The Outsourced State

Storyline for a presentation to the Eighth Central Pay Commission on the contracting-out of government manpower, using Government e-Marketplace (GeM) procurement data, 2019–2025. This is a working draft for review before the slide deck is built.

Prepared for Prakhar Misra · Draft 2 · 22 September 2026 · Source analysis: 8th CPC/GeM_analysis

Reading guideBlue boxes are findings. Yellow boxes are to-dos or decisions for you. Red boxes are caveats that should appear on or under the slide. Every chart has hover values. Each slide section gives the headline message first, then the evidence, then the fine print.

What is new in v2

Draft 1 had the GeM evidence. This draft adds the archival record and the job-by-job costing:

  • S2b — Four Pay Commissions on contracting out (1986–2015), with the paragraphs quoted, from the 4th, 5th, 6th and 7th CPC reports in your folder. The 5th CPC in 1997 listed almost exactly the services now bought on GeM; the 6th CPC in 2008 called outsourcing "a welcome trend that needs to be encouraged".
  • S2c — The regular state stopped growing in 1995, from the Census of Central Government Employees (1971–2011) plus the Pay Research Unit for 2023.
  • S9b — Job-by-job comparison table: 13 outsourced roles, each matched to its regular post and pay level, with the market rate, the cost of the regular post, and the premium at entry and across a career.
  • Defaults used, on the six questions from the last round: framed as a premium rather than a saving; entry and career cost shown; Y-class city; worker take-home included; central posts only; aggregate as a footnote.

1. The pitch in one page

The regular headcount no longer shows who does the government's work. The official count shows 30.6 lakh regular central civilian employees against 40.4 lakh sanctioned posts. It omits a second workforce hired through contractors: sweepers, peons, multi-tasking staff, data entry operators, drivers and guards. These are largely the jobs the Sixth CPC moved out of Group D in 2008. The Seventh CPC was told this workforce cost ₹300 crore a year (2012–13). GeM shows that central ministries, departments and attached offices alone awarded ₹13,455 crore of manpower contracts in 2025, about 4.0 lakh worker-years. Across all public buyers on GeM the figure is ₹39,537 crore.

₹39,537 croreManpower contracts on GeM, 2025 (all public buyers)
₹13,455 croreCentral ministries, departments & attached offices, 2025 (≈45× the ₹300 cr reported to the 7th CPC)
13.5 lakhWorker-year equivalents bought, 2025 (all buyers)
1.6×Cost of a regular Level-1 employee relative to an outsourced unskilled worker

The argument for the Commission has three parts:

  1. Contracting-out is large and growing, and it is not just an artefact of more buying moving onto GeM.
  2. It is concentrated in the jobs at the bottom of the pay matrix, where the government pays well above the market. The gap is about 1.6× at Level 1.
  3. So the Commission's decisions at Levels 1–2 are also decisions about the size of the contract workforce. Every rise in regular pay at the bottom widens the gap and makes contracting-out more attractive. The Commission cannot cost its recommendations or read the vacancy figures properly without counting this workforce.

2. Why the Commission should care

Outsourced workers are not within the ToR's list of employee categories. The pitch therefore cannot be "look, outsourcing". It has to show that these workers shape the things the Commission is asked to decide.

3. Data, fixes & definitions

Source. GeM bid and reverse-auction records scraped from bidplus.gem.gov.in: 2.96 million unique tenders, 2018–2025, with 40.5 million bidder rows. The manpower universe is Tier 1 + Tier 2 of the dissertation classification: named manpower-supply and staffing services, plus housekeeping, facility management and similar outsourced labour services. That is 183,325 tenders for 2019–2025. Tier 3 (transport hire, training) is excluded.

FixWhat was doneResult
F1 · What "quantity" meansThe raw data has no contract-duration or service-charge field. Tested whether per-person price clusters at a loaded minimum-wage rate.63% of contracts fall within ±25% of the skill × year mode. For central buyers in 2025 the unskilled rate is ≈₹24K/month all-in, which matches 12 months at the central minimum wage plus EPF/ESI/bonus/GST. So quantity = persons and the modal term is about 1 year (≈5% are two-year, ≈6% six-month).
F2 · Buyer typeRule-based classification of 1,475 central organisationsMinistries, departments & attached offices 48%; states/UT/local 25%; autonomous bodies 14%; CPSUs 13% of manpower tenders
F3 · Rupee valuesDropped 49 obvious errors (for example ₹2 lakh crore for 16 persons); checked against 99.9% winsorisationClean and winsorised totals agree within about 5%
F4 · GeM adoptionBalanced panel of 507 organisations present on GeM every year 2019–25Manpower grows much faster than their overall GeM use (S5)
F5 · Regular costEntry-pay cost to government at Levels 1, 2, 4, 6L1 ₹4.66 lakh/year, conservative (see S9)
F6 · Clean tableresults/mp_clean.parquet, one row per tenderAll charts below come from it

Worker-year equivalents. Contract value ÷ the median annual cost of one outsourced worker (single-category minimum-wage tenders, by year and central/state buyer). The measure is robust to odd contract lengths and to missing quantities. Applying it to all Tier 1–2 tenders, including facility management that bundles materials, makes it an upper-bound measure of people. The minimum-wage slots figure (the sum of quantities on "Manpower Outsourcing – Minimum wage" tenders) is a lower bound, because it covers only one category.

Caveats that travel with every number(1) GeM covers only procurement routed through GeM. Railways and Home Affairs, 72% of regular civilian staff, buy largely through their own systems (IREPS and others). Direct contractual engagement (consultants, Young Professionals, scheme staff paid from scheme budgets such as NHM) is mostly off GeM. So GeM undercounts the contract workforce. (2) Tenders are awards, not payments made. (3) The data are flows (contracts awarded per year), not a stock of workers on a date. (4) 2026 data are excluded as partial.

Slide 2 · History
Two commissions ago, Group D was abolished. Its work went to contractors, and the 7th CPC was told it cost ₹300 crore.
  • 6th CPC (2008): 9.7 lakh sanctioned Group D posts (8.6 lakh filled). Group D staff were "to be retrained and upgraded" into the lowest Group C grade (para 1.2.9, ch. 3.7). No further Group D recruitment.
  • DoPT to the 7th CPC:
    "with the removal of the category of Group D employees … unskilled functions in the government sector would be contracted from outsources." (7th CPC, para 3.74)
  • 7th CPC (2015): ministries self-reported ₹300.49 crore of annual contractual-manpower expenditure (2012–13, Table 12). The Commission noted it was "likely to increase", recommended "clear guidance … on jobs that can and should be contracted out", model contracts to address "exploitation of contractual manpower", and a standardised personnel database (para 3.83).
  • JCM Staff Side to the 7th CPC: outsourcing is "pushing a major segment of government functions into the informal sector" (para 3.76).
The hookA decade later, GeM data let us answer the question the 7th CPC could not: how large is it, and what jobs does it cover?
Slide 2b · The archive
Four Pay Commissions built this road, one paragraph at a time.
CommissionWhat it saidCitation
4th CPC (1986)Set the boundary that still holds. Casual labourers are "not treated as regular government employees"; following State of Assam v. Kanak Chandra Dutta, a casual labourer "is not the holder of a post under the government". The Commission excluded casual, part-time and contract staff from its purview — and asked ministries for data on "non-regular establishments like work-charged employees, persons employed by government on contract, part-time employees, casual labour and other staff paid from contingency".Part 1, paras 1.15, 2.7
5th CPC (1997)Wrote the outsourcing menu. Under "Contracting out of services": "There is a widening area of services which are currently being performed in-house in the Government, but which can be conveniently outsourced to the private sector" — listing cleanliness, sanitation and housekeeping, maintenance of monuments, lawns and gardens, catering, transport services "to replace the fleet of staff cars", security of non-sensitive buildings, printing and reprography. It also recommended a 30% cut in staff strength and the decasualisation of casual labour.Vol. I, ch. on downsizing, item (e)
6th CPC (2008)Closed the door on Group D and blessed the alternative. Of 9.7 lakh sanctioned Group D posts (8.6 lakh filled), all were to be retrained and upgraded into Group C, with no further recruitment. "Trends in the past have shown that, increasingly, basic work relating to cleaning, security, maintenance, etc. is being outsourced. This is a welcome trend that needs to be encouraged and supported by bringing about systemic changes." Jobs that are not skilled "are either infructuous or can be outsourced". In the central armed police forces it recommended that non-essential jobs done by Followers — peons, farashes, sweepers — "should henceforth be contracted out or outsourced".Paras 3.7.4–3.7.8, 7.19.45, ch. 3.7
7th CPC (2015)Tried to measure it, and could not. Ministries self-reported ₹300.49 crore of annual expenditure on contractual manpower (2012–13). The Commission recorded a "general consensus that routine administrative jobs … can continue to be outsourced", asked for clear guidance on what may be contracted out, model contracts to prevent "exploitation of contractual manpower", and a standardised personnel database. The JCM Staff Side said outsourcing was "pushing a major segment of government functions into the informal sector".Paras 3.72–3.83, Table 12
8th CPC (2025–27)The Terms of Reference do not mention contractual or outsourced personnel at all. The workforce the last three Commissions created is outside the frame of the one now sitting.Gazette Resolution, 3 Nov 2025
The line for the slideEach Commission solved its own problem: the 4th excluded casual labour, the 5th listed what to contract out, the 6th abolished Group D and called outsourcing welcome, the 7th asked for data and got ₹300 crore. Nobody has counted the result. GeM now can.

Slide 2c · The long view
The regular civilian state stopped growing in 1995 and shrank by a quarter.

Regular central government civilian employees, lakh persons. 1971–2011: Census of Central Government Employees (DGE&T), Table 11. 2023: Pay Research Unit. Railways and Defence (civil) shown separately.

FindingRegular employment peaked at 39.8 lakh in 1995 and fell to 30.9 lakh by 2011, where it has stayed (30.6 lakh in 2023). In 2011, 4.86 lakh employees (15.7%) were still in Group D, the category the 6th CPC had abolished three years earlier. Sanctioned posts, meanwhile, stand at 40.4 lakh: the establishment on paper never shrank, the people did — and the gap is what contractors now fill.
CaveatThe Census series ends in 2011 and the PRU series has a different coverage (it excludes some bodies the Census included), so the two are shown as separate markers, not one continuous line.
Slide 3 · Data
GeM is the government's own record of what it buys, including people.

Under GFR 2017 (Rule 149), ministries must buy through GeM the goods and services available on it. Manpower outsourcing is a standard GeM service with fixed parameters: skill level (unskilled / semi-skilled / skilled / highly skilled), education and role. Each tender records the buyer, the number of persons, the winning price and every bidder.

One slide on scope: 183K manpower tenders, 2019–2025, all public buyers. State the caveats from Section 3 in one line.

Manpower contracts on GeM grew from ₹1,987 crore (2019) to ₹39,537 crore (2025).

Awarded contract value by buyer type, ₹ crore, nominal. Tier 1–2 manpower tenders. Hover for values.

People bought: worker-year equivalents (upper bound) and minimum-wage slots (lower bound, one category only; the category appears from 2020).

FindingIn 2025 GeM carried 43,838 manpower tenders, up from 5,818 in 2019. Central ministries, departments and attached offices alone bought ₹13,455 crore, about 4.0 lakh worker-years.

Slide 5 · Robustness
It is not just more buying moving onto GeM: the same buyers outsource more every year.

507 organisations that used GeM in every year 2019–2025. Index, 2021 = 100.

FindingFor the same 507 buyers, 2021→2025: manpower tenders +64%, manpower value +187%, all their GeM tenders +23%. Manpower has been a stable ~30% of all GeM services spending since 2023.

Slide 9 · Cost
An outsourced worker costs the government about 60% of a regular Level-1 employee, and about 45% at Level 6.

Annual cost per person, ₹ lakh. Contract = median GeM price per person, central buyers, 2025 (all-in, including the agency fee and 18% GST; also shown net of GST). Regular = entry pay at the matched level with DA 58%, HRA 20% (Y city), transport allowance, 14% employer NPS and gratuity accrual. CGHS, LTC, leave and bonus are excluded, so the regular cost is understated.

Median contract cost per person per year, central buyers, by skill.

FindingLevel 1 costs ₹4.66 lakh a year versus ₹2.91 lakh for an outsourced unskilled worker (1.6×; 1.9× net of GST, which flows back to the exchequer). At the top of the contract market the gap is wider: a highly-skilled contract worker costs ₹4.09 lakh, against ₹9.17 lakh at Level 6 (2.2×). The contract rate for unskilled work rose +31% from 2021 to 2025, tracking minimum-wage revisions.
To doDA is now 60% (from 1 Jan 2026), but 58% is used here to match 2025 contracts. Add the lifetime cost of a regular post (pension/NPS, gratuity, CGHS) as a second bar. The 7th CPC's IIM-A comparison (government General Helper ₹22,579/month vs ₹8,000–9,500 private) makes a good anchor.
Slide 9b · Job by job
The same job, two ways: what government pays a contractor, and what the regular post costs.

Contract rate: median GeM price per person per year, central buyers, 2023–24 (roles are labelled only up to 2024; see caveat), uprated to 2025 by that skill's central price growth. Includes the agency fee and 18% GST. Regular cost: 7th CPC entry pay at the matched level, DA 58%, HRA 20% (Y city), transport allowance, 14% employer NPS, 4.81% gratuity accrual — excluding CGHS, LTC, leave encashment and bonus. Career column: average annual cost over 30 years with 3% annual increments, in today's prices.

Premium of the regular post over the market rate for the same work: at entry, and averaged over a career.

FindingFor every role, the regular post costs more: 1.3–2.0× at entry and 2.0–3.1× averaged over a career. The premium is largest for the least skilled work (mali, mazdoor, sweeper) and smallest for drivers, guards and technicians — the same pattern the wage-premium literature finds, and the pay-compression problem in Theme 5 of the Session III note, measured from administrative data rather than survey data.
The worker's sideThe contract worker's gross pay is roughly 50–60% of the regular post's gross salary (for example ₹19,150 vs ₹33,462 a month for a sweeper), before counting job security, pension, CGHS or leave. The difference between what government pays and what the worker receives is the agency fee plus GST.
CaveatFrom 2025 GeM truncates its category names and the specific role label largely disappears, collapsing into "Admin" and "Others". Role-level rates therefore come from 2023–24 and are uprated. Pay levels for some roles (electrician, technician) vary by department between Levels 2 and 5; Level 2 is used, which understates the premium.
To doHave someone check the role → post → level concordance against recruitment rules before this goes in front of the Commission.
Slide 10 · The mechanism
Every rise in regular pay at the bottom of the matrix makes contracting-out more attractive.

Plain version. A department short of staff has two ways to fill a routine job. One is a regular post that takes years to fill through SSC/RRB, costs ≈₹4.7 lakh a year plus a lifetime liability, and needs sanction. The other is a GeM contract that can be placed in weeks, costs ≈₹2.9 lakh, and is paid from office-expenses budgets. The Commission's fitment factor at Levels 1–2 sets the gap between the two.

Two forces narrow the gap: the Labour Codes (the 50% wage rule, social-security coverage) and minimum-wage revisions. One force widens it: DA plus the fitment factor.

To doDraw this as a simple diagram for the deck: two cost lines over time, regular and contract, with the gap labelled "the substitution margin". Possibly add one evidence point: do outsourcing tenders jump after DA hikes or after the 7th CPC's implementation in 2016? GeM starts in 2017, so the 7th CPC itself cannot be tested. Frame as a hypothesis.
Slide 11 · The market
Competition is thinning, and bids pile up at the statutory floor.

Median number of financial bidders per manpower tender.

FindingThe median number of bidders fell from 15 (2019) to 11 (2025). In the dissertation rebuild, about 95% of manpower bid rows sit exactly at the L1 price. Agencies compete down to a zero margin over statutory wages, which raises questions about compliance with wage, EPF and ESI obligations. Repeat bidder pairs recur far more than chance (13 pairs ≥10 times vs 1.2 expected).
ToneFor the Commission, frame this as worker-welfare and compliance risk, echoing the 7th CPC's "exploitation of contractual manpower". Keep the collusion evidence as a one-line footnote.
Slide 12 · Data asks
Count them.
  1. Include contract and outsourced personnel in the minimum personnel-data standard proposed for Session III (employment type as a mandatory field).

  3. Create a distinct budget object head for outsourced manpower. This mirrors the 7th CPC's recommendation of an object head for allowances (para 3.83(vi)).

5. Open questions for Prakhar

Decisions needed before building the deck
  1. States in the headline? They are outside the ToR's employee list but inside ToR 2(f)(iv). The current draft leads with the central ministries figure and shows states separately.
  2. Which people measure? Worker-year equivalents (upper bound, all categories) or minimum-wage slots (lower bound, one category)? The suggestion is to show both as a range: "between X and Y lakh worker-years".
  3. CPSUs: keep them as a comparator (ToR f(v)) or drop them from the central total? Currently they are shown separately and excluded from the "central ministries" figure.
  4. Slide 8 (ratio to regular headcount): keep it with caveats, or cut it?
  5. Anomalies to inspect at record level: MoSPI, MoHUA-CPSU, Uttar Pradesh, the "Pmo" ministry label (DAE units).
  6. Real vs nominal: deflate values by CPI-IW? The per-person costs already show a +30% rise from 2021 to 2025.
  7. Tier 2 (facility management, housekeeping) includes materials and equipment. Keep it in the value totals, or show Tier 1 only as a conservative headline?
